Chapter 3

The Assumptions We Make

Assumptions have a way of becoming part of a relationship without either person realizing how much influence they have gained. A missed phone call, a change in plans, a brief response, or an evening spent differently than expected can become the starting point for an entire explanation that exists only in someone’s mind. Once that explanation takes hold, every action that follows may be interpreted through the same understanding, even when the original assumption was never accurate.

For the two men, this sometimes happened in the ordinary moments of their relationship. They had learned one another’s habits, preferences, and personalities, but familiarity occasionally created the impression that they could predict what the other was thinking. The problem was that knowing someone well did not mean knowing the reason behind every decision, and the more comfortable they became with their own interpretations, the easier misunderstandings could become.

One afternoon, the father received a message explaining that his plans for later that evening had changed. His son’s schedule required his attention, and he needed to make an adjustment to the time he and his partner had expected to spend together. He sent a quick message to explain, believing that the information was straightforward and that his partner would understand.

His partner read the message while going about his own afternoon. He understood that parenting responsibilities sometimes required adjustments, and he did not question the father’s need to be available for his son. What bothered him was the way the change had been communicated. They had discussed spending the evening together, and the brief message left him feeling as though the decision had already been made without much consideration for their plans.

By the time the father arrived home, his partner had already formed an opinion about the situation. He assumed that the father had not given their plans much thought and that changing the arrangement had been easier than discussing the decision with him. The father, meanwhile, assumed that his partner understood the circumstances and had no reason to believe the change would become a source of frustration.

Neither man had asked the other what he was thinking.

When the subject eventually came up, the conversation began with frustration rather than curiosity.

“You could have talked to me before changing our plans,” his partner said.

“I told you what happened. I had to make arrangements for my son.”

“I understand that. I’m not upset that you have responsibilities as a father. I’m upset that you made the change and expected me to be fine with finding out through a message.”

The father felt that his responsibilities were being questioned, even though that was not what his partner was trying to communicate. His immediate response was to explain why his son’s needs had to come first in that particular situation. His partner already understood that responsibility, but he wanted the father to recognize that communication and consideration still mattered.

The disagreement had developed around an assumption that neither man had taken the time to examine. The partner had interpreted the brief message as evidence that their plans were unimportant, while the father had interpreted the lack of an immediate objection as acceptance of the change. Each man had responded to what he believed the other meant rather than making certain he understood what had actually been communicated.

Assumptions can become especially difficult when they are connected to previous experiences. A person who has repeatedly felt overlooked may interpret an unexpected change as another example of being placed second. Someone who has felt criticized whenever parenting decisions are discussed may interpret a simple question as a challenge to his responsibilities. Although past experiences can help explain why a particular situation feels significant, they do not always provide an accurate explanation of what is happening in the present.

The same difficulty can arise in conversations about faith. A comment made during a discussion of Scripture may be interpreted as personal criticism, even when the person speaking intended to discuss a theological position. Another person may assume that a shared belief means there is no need to explain his perspective, leaving an important part of the conversation misunderstood. Even within an affirming church, where their relationship is welcomed, two people can have different understandings of a sermon, a biblical passage, or what spiritual growth should look like in their individual lives.

The solution is not to stop having opinions or to avoid difficult conversations. Healthy relationships require people to express what they believe, explain what matters to them, and remain willing to reconsider an interpretation when new information becomes available. Asking a question before reaching a conclusion can prevent a misunderstanding from becoming a larger conflict, particularly when the original situation could have been resolved through a simple conversation.

Later that evening, the father returned to the discussion with a better understanding of what his partner had been trying to express. He explained that the change in plans had been necessary, but acknowledged that he could have communicated more thoughtfully. His partner, in turn, recognized that his initial reaction had been influenced by the feeling that their time together was sometimes difficult to protect. Neither needed to deny his own experience to recognize the other’s perspective.

They still had responsibilities to manage, schedules to coordinate, and expectations to discuss, but the conversation helped them distinguish what had actually happened from what each had assumed about the other’s intentions. That distinction gave them something more useful than another disagreement: an opportunity to communicate with greater consideration.

Assumptions are not always created through carelessness or a lack of love. Sometimes they develop because people believe familiarity should make explanations unnecessary, while other times they arise from experiences that have left someone especially sensitive to certain situations. Recognizing those influences requires honesty, particularly when our interpretation of another person’s actions begins shaping how we treat that person.

A relationship cannot grow through assumptions alone. Understanding begins when we become willing to ask what someone meant rather than deciding the answer before the conversation has taken place.
