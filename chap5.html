Chapter Five When Expectations Become Disappointments Every relationship carries expectations, whether people openly discuss them or simply assume the other person understands what matters. Some expectations develop naturally through shared experiences, while others come from personal values, previous relationships, family backgrounds, and individual ideas about what love should look like. The difficulty begins when two people believe they share the same understanding of a relationship without ever taking the time to discover whether their expectations actually match. For the two men, those differences occasionally appeared in ways that seemed insignificant at first. One believed that spending time together meant making room for one another throughout the week, while the other placed greater importance on having certain evenings set aside specifically for their relationship. Both valued their time together, but they expressed that value differently. What one considered a reasonable amount of attention could leave the other feeling that something was missing. The same differences appeared in how they approached responsibilities. The father had obligations connected to his son, work, and other areas of family life, while his partner had his own commitments and personal needs to consider. Neither expected the other to abandon those responsibilities, yet both sometimes assumed that the other understood what would make them feel appreciated and included. When those expectations were not met, disappointment occasionally developed before either person recognized that an important conversation had never taken place. One Saturday, the father had planned to spend part of the afternoon with his son. His partner knew about the arrangement and had no objection to the time they spent together. Earlier in the week, however, the two men had discussed doing something together later that day. His partner had looked forward to the opportunity, imagining that they would have the evening to themselves after the father’s plans were finished. As the afternoon progressed, the father received a message about an additional family matter that required his attention. He decided to adjust the evening accordingly, believing that his partner would understand the change. When he explained the situation, his partner became disappointed, not because he objected to the father’s responsibilities, but because he had been looking forward to something they had discussed and had not expected the plans to change again. “I understand that things come up,” his partner said, “but sometimes I feel like I am the part of the schedule that gets moved whenever something else needs attention.” The father did not agree with that interpretation. He believed he had been doing his best to balance everything, and he did not want his responsibilities as a parent to be viewed as an inconvenience to their relationship. From his perspective, he was responding to circumstances that required his attention, not deliberately placing his partner last. “I am not trying to make you feel that way. I have responsibilities, and sometimes plans have to change.” “I know that. I’m not asking you to ignore those responsibilities. I just want us to be able to make plans and have some confidence that our time together matters too.” The conversation revealed that their disagreement involved more than one changed evening. His partner wanted to feel that their relationship received intentional attention, while the father wanted his efforts and responsibilities to be understood rather than interpreted as a lack of commitment. Both expectations were understandable, although neither had fully explained what the other needed to recognize. Disappointment often develops when the difference between what someone expects and what actually happens becomes a measure of how much that person believes he matters. A cancelled plan can begin to feel like rejection, while an unanswered request can become evidence, in someone’s mind, that his needs are not important. Those conclusions may reflect genuine concerns, but they can also become unfair when a person assumes that every unmet expectation carries the same meaning. This does not mean people should lower their expectations until nothing matters. Healthy relationships require consideration, reliability, affection, and a willingness to make room for one another. The important distinction is between expectations that have been communicated and mutually understood, and expectations that one person believes should be obvious simply because the relationship exists. Their circumstances also required an understanding that the father’s relationship with his son could not be measured by the same expectations as his romantic relationship. His son needed his father’s attention and commitment, while his partner needed a meaningful place within their shared life. Those needs were different, and neither relationship needed to compete with the other. The challenge was to communicate honestly about schedules, priorities, and the time available without making the child responsible for adult disappointment or placing the partner in a parental position he had never claimed. Faith offered another perspective on expectations. Both men attended a church where their relationship was welcomed, and they valued the opportunity to worship and grow alongside other believers. Still, they had to recognize that being part of an affirming congregation did not make them immune to the ordinary challenges of living out their faith. Following Jesus involved personal growth, humility, forgiveness, and accountability, including the way they treated one another when expectations were not met. Their commitment to Christian principles needed to be reflected in everyday conduct, not simply in what they professed to believe. Eventually, the two men began discussing what intentional time together actually meant to each of them. The father explained that some responsibilities could not always be scheduled in advance, while his partner acknowledged that flexibility was necessary when family circumstances changed. They also recognized that making plans required more than good intentions; both needed to communicate what they could realistically commit to and make an effort to protect the time they agreed to share. The conversation did not eliminate every disappointment, nor did either man promise that circumstances would always unfold as expected. What changed was their understanding that expectations deserved to be discussed rather than silently measured, and that disappointment should become an opportunity for communication instead of an immediate judgment about the relationship. Love does not require two people to anticipate every need without being told, and commitment does not guarantee that every plan will unfold perfectly. A healthy relationship develops when both people are willing to explain what matters, consider what is reasonable, and recognize the difference between an unavoidable change and a repeated pattern of disregard. Expectations become healthier when they are communicated, understood, and shared. Without that understanding, even people who genuinely love one another can find themselves disappointed by promises the other person never realized they had made.
