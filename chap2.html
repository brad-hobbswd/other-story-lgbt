Chapter 2

Different Experiences, Different Realities

One of the most difficult things about sharing a life with another person is recognizing that two people can experience the same circumstances and come away with completely different understandings of what happened. Neither person necessarily intends to misunderstand the other, and neither may realize how differently the moment was experienced until a conversation brings those differences into the open.

For the two men, this had become familiar in some of the smaller details of their everyday lives. One might consider an evening spent at home a welcome opportunity to relax after a demanding day, while the other might have looked forward to spending that same evening talking, laughing, and enjoying time together. The plans had not changed, and neither person had done anything particularly wrong, yet their expectations had been different from the beginning.

One Friday evening, the father arrived home feeling exhausted. His day had involved responsibilities that required his attention, and he was looking forward to settling down for the night. His partner, however, had been anticipating an evening together and had already imagined how they might spend their time. When the father changed into comfortable clothes and settled onto the couch, his partner initially assumed they would have an opportunity to enjoy one another’s company.

As the evening continued, the father became absorbed in his phone and eventually turned on the television. His partner tried to start a conversation, but the responses were brief, and the attention he hoped to receive never quite materialized. From the father’s perspective, there was nothing unusual about the evening because he believed they were simply relaxing together. His partner experienced something different, feeling as though being in the same room had somehow replaced actually spending time together.

Later, his partner brought up the subject.

“I thought we were going to spend some time together tonight,” he said.

“We are spending time together. We’ve been sitting here all evening.”

“I know we’re in the same room, but that’s not exactly what I meant. I wanted us to talk and actually enjoy being together.”

The father looked surprised by the comment because he had not considered the evening a disappointment. He had been physically present, had not made other plans, and believed that sharing a quiet evening at home was a reasonable way to spend time together. His partner was not questioning whether he had been home; he was trying to explain that the kind of attention he had hoped for was missing.

This is where different experiences can create different realities within the same relationship. The father measured the evening by the time they had shared, while his partner measured the experience by the connection he had felt during that time. Both descriptions were honest, yet neither one completely represented the other’s experience.

Their differences did not mean that one man loved the other less, nor did they establish that one was right and the other was wrong. The father genuinely needed rest, while his partner genuinely wanted connection. The difficulty arose when each assumed that the other understood what the evening meant to him without having clearly communicated those expectations.

The same principle can appear in many areas of a relationship, including parenting schedules, household responsibilities, church activities, finances, and decisions about the future. One partner may see a particular arrangement as practical and necessary, while the other experiences the arrangement as another occasion when his needs have been placed second. A father may be focused on fulfilling his responsibilities to his child, while his partner may be trying to understand where their shared time fits within those responsibilities. Neither experience should automatically be dismissed, although the responsibilities of parenthood must remain clear and the child’s well-being must never become a bargaining point between adults.

Faith can also be experienced differently by two people who attend the same church, believe in Jesus, and share similar convictions. One may find encouragement in a particular message, while the other connects the message to a personal question he has been carrying for years. Sharing a church community does not mean every sermon, conversation, or spiritual experience will have the same meaning for both people. Their faith may be shared, but their individual journeys remain personal.

Learning to recognize those differences changes the way conversations happen. Rather than immediately explaining why an action was reasonable, each person can begin by considering what the other experienced and why the moment carried such significance. Understanding another person’s reality does not require abandoning personal needs or accepting an inaccurate account of what happened. It simply creates room for a more complete conversation, one in which both people have an opportunity to explain themselves without being reduced to their intentions or their reactions.

The two men eventually found a better way to discuss their expectations for evenings together. The father explained that some days he needed time to unwind before having a meaningful conversation, while his partner explained that he did not need every evening to be planned or elaborate; he simply wanted moments when they were genuinely attentive to one another. Their discussion did not eliminate the differences between them, but it helped them understand what each had been asking for.

Relationships become stronger when people stop assuming that their own experience automatically explains the experience of someone else. What feels like enough to one person may leave another feeling disconnected, and what appears insignificant from one perspective may carry considerable meaning from another. The goal is not to decide whose reality deserves to matter, but to understand how both experiences can be acknowledged while taking responsibility for the choices that shape the relationship.

Sharing a life means learning that two people do not have to experience every moment in the same way, but they do need to make room for each other’s experiences when building a life together.
