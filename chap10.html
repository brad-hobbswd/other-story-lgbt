Chapter Ten Understanding Effort and Intent One of the most painful experiences in a relationship is believing that everything you have been doing should be enough, only to discover that the person you love has been feeling neglected for longer than you realized. The person making the effort may feel unappreciated, while the person waiting to receive that effort may feel unseen. Both can be telling the truth about their experiences, yet neither may fully understand why the other continues to feel disappointed. For the two men, this difference became increasingly difficult to ignore. The father believed he was making a genuine effort to maintain their relationship while balancing work, parenting, household responsibilities, and the ordinary demands of life. He made time when he could, included his partner in plans, checked in throughout the day, and tried to be present even when his schedule left him exhausted. From his perspective, those actions demonstrated commitment. He was not ignoring the relationship, and he certainly did not believe his partner was unimportant. His partner saw some of those efforts, but he also experienced something that was difficult to explain without sounding ungrateful. He appreciated the messages, the shared evenings, and the moments when they could spend time together, yet he sometimes felt that he was receiving whatever attention remained after the rest of life had taken priority. He did not expect to be placed above the father’s responsibilities as a parent, nor did he want to compete with the needs of a child. What he wanted was the reassurance that their relationship was being intentionally cared for rather than simply maintained whenever circumstances allowed. The difference became painfully clear one evening when the father mentioned how much he had been doing for their relationship. “I don’t understand what more you want from me,” he said. “I work, I take care of my responsibilities, I make time for us, and I try to show you that I love you. Sometimes I feel like nothing I do is enough.” His partner looked at him for a moment before responding. “Do you really think I don’t see what you do?” “Sometimes, yes. You keep telling me that you need more, but you never seem to recognize what I’m already giving.” “I recognize it. What hurts is that you think recognizing your effort means I should stop telling you that something is missing.” The response caught the father off guard. He had been measuring his commitment through the things he could identify and count, while his partner had been trying to describe the emotional experience those actions were creating. The father heard the complaint as a rejection of his efforts, while his partner experienced his defensiveness as another refusal to understand what he had been trying to communicate. The conversation became more intense when his partner explained that he was tired of feeling guilty for wanting attention from the person he loved. “I don’t want to have to prove that I deserve time with you,” he said. “I don’t want every conversation about us to turn into a list of everything you have already done. I’m not asking you to abandon your responsibilities. I’m asking you to understand that I have responsibilities in this relationship too, and one of them is telling you when I’m hurting.” The father felt the weight of those words, although part of him still wanted to defend himself. He knew how much he had sacrificed to keep everything moving, and he did not want those sacrifices dismissed. His partner, however, was not asking him to deny his effort. He was asking him to consider whether the effort being offered was reaching the place where connection was needed. That distinction matters because intention and impact are not always the same. Someone may intend to show love by providing stability, handling responsibilities, or making sure practical needs are met. The other person may need affection, meaningful conversation, reassurance, or the feeling that time together is being protected. Neither form of love is automatically more valuable, but neither should be used to dismiss the needs of the other. Understanding effort also requires honesty about what a person can reasonably give. No relationship can demand unlimited availability, and genuine commitment does not mean one person must sacrifice every personal need to keep another person satisfied. The father had legitimate responsibilities that could not simply be rearranged whenever his partner wanted more time. His partner also had legitimate emotional needs that could not be dismissed whenever the father felt overwhelmed. Their relationship needed room for both realities, along with a willingness to discuss what each could change. Their faith offered another opportunity for reflection. Both men valued their relationship with Jesus Christ and believed their conduct should reflect the love, patience, humility, and accountability they sought to practice. Attending an affirming church gave them a place where their relationship was welcomed, but their shared faith did not remove the ordinary responsibility of learning how to love one another well. Religious language could not replace a sincere apology, and being able to speak about love could never excuse a repeated failure to demonstrate consideration. The father eventually began asking a different question. Rather than wondering why his partner could not see everything he was doing, he started considering whether he had understood what his partner was actually asking for. His partner also began recognizing that the father’s efforts were genuine, even when those efforts did not always meet the emotional need being expressed. Neither realization settled every disagreement, but both created room for a more honest conversation. They began making changes that were specific rather than simply promising to do better. The father became more intentional about setting aside time when possible, while his partner made an effort to express his needs without treating every disappointment as evidence that he was unwanted. They still had responsibilities, occasional frustrations, and moments when their expectations differed, but they were learning to evaluate their relationship through more than the intentions each person believed he had. A person can love sincerely and still have something important to learn about how that love is received. Likewise, someone can feel deeply disappointed without recognizing the effort being made on his behalf. Neither experience should silence the other. Understanding begins when both people become willing to look beyond their own intentions long enough to consider the emotional impact of their actions. Love should not require one person to deny his effort or the other to deny his hurt. A healthy relationship makes room for both truths, then asks what each person is willing to do differently so that love can be experienced rather than merely intended.
